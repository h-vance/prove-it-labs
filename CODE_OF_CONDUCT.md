# Code of conduct

This course exists for people who bounce off setup friction, walls of prose, and
rooms where everyone else already knows the vocabulary. That includes a lot of
very good engineers, and it sets the standard for how this repository is run.

## What is expected

**Assume the question is reasonable.** Somebody asking why an exercise fails
when they think they fixed it is giving you a bug report. Somebody asking what a
command does is telling you the material has a gap. Both are useful.

**Answer the question that was asked.** Not the one you would rather answer, and
not the one that lets you demonstrate something adjacent.

**Be specific rather than clever.** "Read the error" helps nobody. Which line,
and what it means, helps everybody.

**Disagree about the work.** Whether a ticket gives away its layer, whether a
grader accepts a fix it should not, whether a scrub rule hides real drift: argue
about these freely and with evidence. That is the entire point.

## What is not

Harassment, insults, and demeaning comments. Sexualized language or imagery.
Publishing anyone's private information. Sustained disruption. Treating somebody
as less serious because of who they are, what they do not yet know, where they
work, or how they got here.

Gatekeeping counts. So does answering a beginner's question in a way designed to
make them feel it was a stupid one.

## Reporting

Contact the maintainer through GitHub. Reports are read by the maintainer only and are
not shared further without your agreement.

Say what happened and link it if it is public. You do not need to have decided
whether it was serious enough. That is not your job to work out on your own.

Responses run from a private word through to editing or removing content and
blocking accounts, depending on what happened. Whatever the outcome, you will
hear back rather than being left wondering whether the message landed.

This applies in issues, pull requests, discussions, and commit messages, and to
anywhere somebody is representing this project.
